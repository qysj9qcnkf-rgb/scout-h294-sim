Does Mia get more out of a shopper when she already knows what they did on the store?

Yes, with one line softened: on HeliumDeploy's real 60-word calls Mia with the shopper's history is now level with today's Mia (2.15 vs 2.21 of 4 hidden truths, within noise) and a blind side-by-side judge preferred her calls 19 to 12, and on longer 150-word calls she leans ahead (3.08 vs 2.96; side by side 14 to 6), though neither gain is big enough to be sure.

On HeliumDeploy, Mia introduces herself as Sarah, so the transcripts say Sarah.

What we tested

  • 24 invented shoppers on HeliumDeploy's real catalog: 10 buyers, 7 who left a checkout, 7 who browsed a long time without buying. Each has 4 hidden truths: why they bought or left, the competitor they compared, how they feel about the store, and what they already buy elsewhere.
  • Each shopper was interviewed by Mia without and with their store history. Without = Mia's call setup on main today (an emailed buyer's first name and latest item are already known). With = the same setup from PR #648, which adds their past orders, a checkout they left, and what they looked at and carted this visit. Both prompts were built by each version's real code on HeliumDeploy's live settings (goals, intro, 10% reward, 60-word minimum).
  • Text only. Mia runs on the live call model (Claude Sonnet 5.5), with her real tools and her real close: the call ends once the shopper has said the word minimum. A separate model plays the shopper: natural, a bit guarded, and gives a truth only when asked something it answers.
  • Two blind judges (Claude Opus 5.5), neither told which version it was reading: one scored each call against the hidden truths (with the shopper's quote) and flagged reading out history, "I can see your cart", wrong facts and anything creepy; the other read both calls of a shopper side by side and counted everything the owner learns.

The fix: 60 words with one line softened

The first version's history line told Mia her first question should build on the history, which used up her only follow-up. The softened line, for calls under 100 words only, lets the history shape her opening and asks her to spend each follow-up on a different goal (why, who else they compared, how they feel about the store, what they buy elsewhere). The with-history version reran twice against the same without-history calls; 150-word calls keep the first line, so their prompts are unchanged.

What it tells us

  • At 60 words Mia gets one follow-up question, and the first version changed what she spent it on. PR #648 tells her that her "first question after the opening line builds on the most telling thing here". With a long-visit shopper she then asks about the product decision (noise, the Q versus the Mini 3) instead of how they feel about the store or who else they compared: with history, the store feeling came out in 14% of long-visit calls against 43% without, and the competitor in 79% against 93%. The softened line fixes most of that: long-visit shoppers now go 2 better, 2 same, 3 worse instead of 1 / 0 / 6.
  • At 150 words the history leans positive, with no clear cause. The side-by-side judge picked the with-history call for all 7 people who left a checkout, but for 4 of them the history adds nothing Mia didn't already have (no past orders; main already names the item they left), Mia built a question on their history in only 2 of the 7 calls, and the same group went the other way at 60 words (3 better, 7 worse). Read it as a lean, not proof.
  • No creepiness problem. Across 72 calls with history, the judge flagged one mild moment ("Earlier you looked at the Avalon Mini 3 too", accurate, the shopper didn't mind) and nothing read out like a record. Without history, Mia once got a fact wrong ("now that it's plugged in", the order hadn't shipped); with history she didn't.
  • The bigger lever is the word minimum, not the history. How the shopper feels about the store came out in 17% of 60-word calls and 88% of 150-word calls. What they buy elsewhere stayed rare in both (12% at 150 words): Mia hardly ever asks it.

What happens next

  • PR #648 now carries the softened line (short calls only) and goes up for review. Shipping still needs Emile's go.
  • Worth a separate test: a higher word minimum on HeliumDeploy. Going from 60 to 150 words took the store feeling from 17% to 88% of calls, a far bigger gain than the history gives.

Fair test? The limits

  • The shoppers are invented (nobody real), and one model plays all of them; real people are messier. Text, not voice. Each simulated answer is 8 to 30 words, an assumption not checked against real HeliumDeploy calls; longer real answers would make 60-word calls even shorter.
  • Both versions ran at the model's default sampling: Sonnet 5.5 accepts no temperature or seed, so a run is not exactly repeatable. HeliumDeploy's 60-word call ran twice per version to even that out.
  • The "without history" version already knows an emailed buyer's first name and latest item (that's main today), so for buyers PR #648 adds only older orders, statuses and a checkout left since.
  • The truth judge gives partial answers the benefit of the doubt (e.g. "I don't get the tech stuff" counted once as a feeling, once not), the same way for both versions.
  • 24 shoppers detect a difference of about half a truth per call; smaller effects would need more shoppers.
  • API cost of the whole test: about $15.50 (first run about $12, the softened rerun about $3.50). Nothing in production changed.
